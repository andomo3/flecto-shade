# Why is one roof, one decision the wrong size, and what is the general problem a bed by bed roof answers?

## Answer

The team lead is right, and the fix is small.
The problem is not "three crops".
The problem is that the light a bed needs changes with the species, with the stage of the same crop, and with the season, while a shade house gives every bed one fixed percentage of shade.

The most general statement that the sources defend:

> The University of Florida's own table for foliage growers near Apopka lists 50 crops whose recommended shade runs from 30 to 90 percent, and tells growers to change the light on the same plant for months before sale, yet a shade house gives every bed under it one fixed number.

A shorter form for the table: "The right light depends on the crop, its stage, and the season, and shade cloth is one number for the whole house, all year."

Both are built from quotes opened on 2026-09-19 and nothing in them is the team's own figure.

### The hero scenarios, ranked

1. **One foliage nursery, one roof, beds at different stages.**
   A grower of weeping fig or schefflera grows the young plants bright for trunk and shape, then must hold them under 70 percent shade or more for 2 to 6 months before sale, beside beds of shade crops that stay near 80 percent all the time.
   Today that means a second structure and moving the plants, or growing everything in shade and giving up the form buyers like.
   With a roof that decides per bed, the bed changes and the plants stay put.
   Sources: Conover and Poole, CFREC-A RH-90-1, and the Ficus Production Guide RH-91-16, both from the Apopka centre.
   This is the most realistic, because it is the normal practice of the local industry, written by the local research centre, and it answers the judge who says "just build two houses".
2. **One foliage nursery, two or three light classes from one UF table.**
   Keep a mixed house, and take every crop from Table 1 of RH-90-1: for example Aglaonema or Spathiphyllum at 80 percent shade, Boston fern at 70 to 80, and weeping fig at 40 to 60 or croton at 30 to 70.
   All are signature crops of the place and all come from one table, so no judge can call the mix invented.
   Three zones still works on the screen, but the words become "three light classes", not "three crops".
   The hydrangea and the blueberry go, because shaded blueberry is not Florida practice, which H1 already admits.
3. **One crop, one year: fixed cloth is wrong in both seasons.**
   UF's croton circular says "30% shade is not quite enough in summer, but 47% is too much in winter."
   A roof holding a daily light target is right in both.
   This is the easiest to show with the simulated year, but be blunt about it: season alone justifies a moving roof, not a bed sized one, and a retractable screen already answers it.
   Use it as the supporting beat, never as the hero.

### What to stop saying

- "A grower with three crops" as the premise.
  Say "beds at different stages and in different light classes".
- Any hint that growers cannot cope today.
  They cope by picking one cloth per house, by building more than one house, and by moving plants.
  No source was found that prices that, so the team may name the practice and must not put a cost on it.
- Any hint that most foliage crops need different roofs.
  In the UF table 25 of the 50 crops sit at "70 - 80%" or "80%", so a grower of those alone is served well by one cloth.
  The need for zones is real for growers who mix light classes or who acclimatize, and that is the honest scope.

Found by a research agent that opened every source on 2026-09-19.
Anything it saw only in a search snippet is marked unverified and is kept out of the verdicts.

## Findings

### 1. Different light by species

- Conover and Poole, CFREC-Apopka Research Report RH-90-1, Table 1, "Suggested light and nutritional levels for production of some potted acclimatized foliage plants in Florida", gives light in foot candles and shade cloth in percent for 50 crops.
- The extremes: Aphelandra squarrosa "1000-1500" foot candles at "80 - 90%" shade, against Araucaria heterophylla and Ficus elastica at "4000-8000" foot candles and "30 - 60%" shade.
- Counted from that table by this agent: 19 crops at "70 - 80%", 8 at "60 - 80%", 6 at "80%", 5 at "40 - 70%", 3 at "50 - 70%", 2 each at "30 - 60%", "40 - 60%", and "40 - 80%", and 1 each at "80 - 90%", "30 - 70%", and "50 - 80%".
- Crops in it that the team already uses or lists as backups: Nephrolepis exaltata "1500-3000" and "70 - 80%", Aglaonema "1000-2500" and "80%", Dieffenbachia "1500-2500" and "70 - 80%", Ficus benjamina "4000-6000" and "40 - 60%", Codiaeum, the croton, "3000-8000" and "30 - 70%".
- The same report sorts plants into classes: "extreme shade plants, those that require moderate to heavy shade to produce attractive plants and cannot be acclimatized to high light", such as "Aglaonema, Maranta and Spathiphyllum", and "sun-shade" plants such as "Brassaia, Ficus and Dracaena".
- UF/IFAS on anthurium: "Typical light levels range from 1,000 to 2,500 foot candles or 90% to 80% shade in Florida", and more light "can cause pale leaf color, which is often misdiagnosed as a nutrient deficiency."
- UF/IFAS on Boston fern: it grows well at "1,500 to 3,000 ft-c, with best quality usually produced near 2,000 ft-c", and "Too much light causes fronds to become light green in color. Light intensity that is too low results in elongated, weak fronds".
- How many species one nursery grows: not found.
  The Ficus guide says Florida nurserymen "have listed some 43 different species and cultivars of Ficus" over 20 years, which shows the width of one genus across the industry, not of one grower.
- Whether growers build separate houses at 47, 63, 73, or 80 percent for different species: no primary source was found that says so in words.
  The table gives a shade cloth percent per crop, and the research reports test cloth at 30, 47, 55, 63, and 80 percent, which is consistent with one cloth per structure, and no more than that may be said.
- "More than 500 plant species" grown as foliage plants was seen in a search snippet only and was not found on the page when opened, so it is unverified.

### 2. Different light by growth stage, for the same crop

- RH-90-1: "foliage plants can be light-acclimatized in two ways: 1) plants can be grown under a specific shade level for their entire production period, or 2) they can be grown under high light or, in some cases, full sun and then converted to low light at some period during production."
- Why growers choose the second way: "Ficus cultivars which produce heavier caliper trunks and more compact crowns in full sun", and "Many consumers also prefer the shape of Araucaria and Brassaia grown in full sun."
- How long the conversion takes: "Most plants require 8 weeks to 12 months or more to convert, depending on size."
- The Ficus Production Guide, RH-91-16: "Most figs can be grown in full sun, but all finished plants for use indoors should be acclimatized under reduced light intensity."
- The same guide: "Plants should be exposed to shade levels of 70% or more (3000 or less ft-c) for a minimum of 2 to 6 months depending on plant size".
- What goes wrong without it: such plants "are not light acclimatized and usually defoliate excessively when moved to interior conditions."
- The review of Ficus benjamina acclimatization, RH-91-5: "Eight weeks of full sun followed by 16 weeks of 63% shade produced plants with thicker trunks but otherwise of the same quality as those grown in shade for the entire 24 weeks."
- The same review: plants held well indoors "if they were first acclimatized for at least 5 weeks under 1,250 to 2,500 ft-c (60% to 80% shade)."
- So yes: the same plants need a different roof in different weeks, and the two light levels are far apart, full sun against 63 to 80 percent shade.
- The limit the sources set: RH-90-1 says "we suggest shade growing for all plants in 10-inch (3 gallon) containers and smaller", and "Most Ficus benjamina grown today are acclimatized during production under shade, the exception being large specimen trees".
  So the sun then shade route is real and is the minority route, used for larger plants.

### 3. Different light by season and by hour

- UF/IFAS Circular ENH878 on croton: "Suggested shade percentages for stem production in Florida are 30 to 47%. For most cultivars, 30% shade is not quite enough in summer, but 47% is too much in winter."
  This is the single best quote found, because the extension service itself says neither fixed cloth is right all year.
- UF/IFAS on Boston fern: "During winter months, low light and cool temperature conditions can cause problems for young, recently potted plants", and "During the summer, high heat and high light intensity may need to be controlled. Light intensity can be reduced in the greenhouse by installing a 30% to 60% shade fabric."
- RH-90-1 ties feeding to the light the cloth lets through: under higher light "the suggested fertilizer levels will have to be increased by 50% or more", and under lower light they "can be reduced by as much as 25%."
- The old Croton Production Guide's line that the light wanted "can be obtained with 50 to 63% shadecloth depending on season" was seen in a search snippet only, and the page would not open, so it is unverified.
- The labour of changing cloth by season: no figure found.
  The nearest opened source is UF/IFAS EP429 on cold protection, which says a retractable curtain "is expensive initially, but it is less labor intensive than applying and removing seasonal shadehouse cladding every year."
  That is about winter film, not shade cloth, and is an analogy only.
- Whitewash applied and removed by season is already in the market file, from ReduSystems, and was not reopened.

### 4. Non-uniformity the grower does not choose

- Water, overhead irrigation: Alabama Extension, Krofft and Pickens, 2020: "A DU of 80 percent or more is considered a best management practice", and in a 2019 audit "All nine of the nurseries visited fell below the BMP guidelines of 80 percent or more."
- The same audit recorded distribution uniformity of 66, 72, and 78 percent at three nurseries, and at the poorest "some areas in the block received 0.1 inches of irrigation while others received 0.7 inches."
- Its consequence: "some plants may be underwatered, while others may receive excess water. This can lead to issues with plant uniformity and losses due to drought stress or disease pressure", with leaching fractions in one nursery from 5.0 to 67.2 percent.
- Water, pot to pot: Daniels, Barnard, Chapman and Bauerle, HortScience 47(1):98-104, 2012, on 10 tree species in a container nursery.
  To know a species' mean substrate water content within plus or minus 5 percent, "The maximum value, ranging from 11 to 20 sensors" was needed on the most variable day, and "The mean value, ranging from six to 10 sensors among species".
  "Seasonal differences in within-species variation were also evident in nine of the 10 species."
- Light and microclimate: Brien et al., Plant Methods, 2013, on a research greenhouse.
  It cites evidence that "small-scale spatial variability in photosynthetically active radiation in a gable-roof greenhouse is demonstrated."
  Its own wheat plants of one variety differed by place: the coefficient of variation of plant area at day 21 was 19.3, 20.3, and 25.8 percent in three locations of one greenhouse, and the south-east location averaged less than the north-west.
  That is variation in the plants, not a light reading, and it is one research house.
- A published figure for light under gutters and roof openings, a drop "larger by 15-28%" below roof openings, was seen in a search snippet from Biosystems Engineering only, and the page returned an error, so it is unverified.
- Temperature: UF/IFAS EP429 says "If temperatures in the greenhouse or shadehouse are not uniform during cold events, then readings should be taken in the spots that are typically coldest", and "Most growers are usually aware that some areas of their property are more prone to cold damage than others."
- Rain and irrigation under shade: RH-90-1 says "Leaching of the potting medium is more difficult to control for plants grown in the open or under shade", and "Irrigation scheduling must factor in rainfall since addition of an irrigation sequence on already saturated media will leach fertilizer and possibly contribute to ground water pollution."
  This is the best support found for the soil and rain rule, and it comes from the Apopka centre.

### 5. Labour and flexibility

- AmericanHort with UC Davis and Michigan State, the 2022 Greenhouse and Nursery Labor Employment Survey: "Nearly two thirds of survey respondents indicated that they were unable to hire all the employees they wanted during 2021."
- The same survey: "The average workforce shortage across the sample was nearly 20% of the workforce that growers needed to operate at full capacity", and "Nearly half of the surveyed growers started using a new labor-saving technology during 2021."
- Fulcher et al., HortScience 58(12):1513-1525, 2023, read through the USDA ARS record of the paper: "more than 65% of surveyed U.S. nurseries increased worker wages and more than 55% of respondents adopted automation to address the labor shortage."
- The same paper: "production tasks excluding irrigation were on average 34% automated or mechanized at container and field nurseries", and some nurseries are "reducing production of labor-intensive plants, or delaying expansion plans."
- USDA NASS, 2024 Census of Horticultural Specialties, highlights: labour expenses of 5,571 million dollars out of 14,919 million in total, which this agent computes as 37 percent, with 248,827 hired workers.
- The same census: 360 million square feet under shade structures in 2024, down 8 percent from 392 million in 2019, against 866 million under greenhouses, and "Florida remains the state with the most horticultural operations at 1,779."
  Foliage sales were 932 million dollars, up 35 percent.
- The labour of moving plants between light environments, or of changing cloth: not found as a figure in any source opened.
  The sources say plants are "converted to low light" and warn about plants "pulled from shaded greenhouses", so the movement is real, and it is not quantified.
- Florida's share of national foliage sales: Chen, Henny and McConnell, 2002, say Florida accounts "for more than 55% of the national wholesale value since the 1960s."
  A figure of 74 percent for 2019 was seen in a search snippet only and is unverified.

### 6. Precedent for zoned shading at fine scale

- No source was found that describes a moving shade divided by bed or bench in commercial practice.
- The nearest opened source is Stamps, HortScience 44(2), 2009, from the Apopka centre, on coloured shade nets.
  Nets are chosen by crop and installed in blocks, and the results vary by crop: "the effects are varied and plant responses may differ even among cultivars of the same plant."
- One finding in it touches the blueberry: in Chile "35% gray, 50% red, and 50% white nets increased yields of highbush blueberry cultivar Berkeley by 60% to 91%" in year one, and by 26 to 45 percent in year two.
  That is Chile and highbush, and it does not make shaded blueberry a Florida practice.
  The copy opened is hosted by a net vendor, and the journal's own page refused the request.
- Brien et al. describe a conveyor greenhouse where "the conveyor system allows for plant locations to be rotated during an experiment", and conclude that moving plants to even out microclimate is no better than good design.
  The industry's fine scale answer so far is to move the plant, not the roof.
- Rolling benches under different screens, split curtains by bay, and bench level shading: not found in any primary source opened.
  This is an absence in these searches and not proof, so the words stay "we found no example", as in the market file.

Nothing was CONTRADICTED outright.
Two things come close, and a judge who knows horticulture will raise them.

- Half of UF's foliage table is one shade class, so a specialised grower is served well by one cloth, and the honest customer is the grower who mixes classes or who acclimatizes.
- The season problem is already answered at house scale by retractable screens, so it cannot carry the claim about size.
