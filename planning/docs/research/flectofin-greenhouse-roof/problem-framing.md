# What is the most general problem this roof answers that the sources will back, and which wider claims have to be dropped?

## Answer

The general problem, in one sentence: what a plant needs depends on the state of its own bed, its crop, its stage, the season, and how wet its soil is, and the roof is the one part of the system that cannot act at that size.
That sentence is backed four ways by the University of Florida's own centre at Apopka and by the FAO, and it does not need three crops to be true.
Three wider claims the team lead proposed do not survive the sources and are dropped: that irrigation infrastructure is wasted, that the roof saves motor energy, and that it cuts spending on artificial lighting.
One finding changes the rain story: a cloth shade house already lets all the rain through, so the roof's new act is keeping a storm off a bed that is already wet, and the Apopka centre's own words say why that matters.

This note is a synthesis of three research notes in this folder, each written by a research agent that opened its sources on 2026-09-19:
`problems-one-roof-one-decision.md`, `problems-water-and-rain.md`, and `problems-energy-light-and-heat.md`.
The planner then opened the three sources this note leans on hardest and checked the quotes word for word, and those are marked "checked" below.
Every other quote is in the three notes, and the notes say which came through a page reader and need one more look before they go on a slide.

## The four legs the general problem stands on

### 1. The right light depends on the crop

- Conover and Poole, University of Florida CFREC-Apopka Research Report RH-90-1, Table 1, lists 50 foliage crops with their shade.
  Checked: the table runs from "30 - 60%" to "80 - 90%", and 25 of the 50 sit at "70 - 80%" or "80%".
- Checked, from the same table: Aglaonema "80%", Spathiphyllum "80%", Nephrolepis exaltata, the Boston fern, "70 - 80%", Ficus benjamina "40 - 60%", Codiaeum variegatum, the croton, "30 - 70%".
- The honest limit: half the table shares one shade class, so a grower who specialises is served by one cloth.
  The problem belongs to the grower with more than one light class, and the pitch says so.

### 2. The right light depends on the season, for the same crop

- Checked, Stamps and Osborne, UF/IFAS Circular ENH878, page 3, both authors at the Mid-Florida Research and Education Center, Apopka: "Suggested shade percentages for stem production in Florida are 30 to 47%. For most cultivars, 30% shade is not quite enough in summer, but 47% is too much in winter."
- The honest limit: season alone argues for shade that moves, which growers can already buy.
  It argues for a bed sized roof only together with leg 1 or leg 3.
- UF marks this circular as an archival copy, from 2003.

### 3. The right light depends on the stage, for the same plant

- The Apopka centre's acclimatization work has growers raise a plant bright for form and then hold it under heavy shade before sale.
  The Ficus production guide gives "shade levels of 70% or more" for "a minimum of 2 to 6 months", in the research note, not checked by the planner.
- Today that means a second structure and moving the plants.
  With a roof that decides by the bed, the bed changes and the plants stay.
- The honest limit, which the sources set themselves: it is the route for larger plants, and the guide suggests shade growing throughout for containers of 10 inches and smaller.

### 4. Rain is only worth something on a bed that is dry

- Checked, FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 25, Dastane 1974: "When a shower falls just after irrigation, it becomes surplus water and is lost through deep percolation or run-off, but if the soil is dry it is recharged with moisture, resulting in a saving in irrigation water."
  This is the roof's soil rule, stated as doctrine.
- Checked, Conover and Poole RH-90-1, the same Apopka report: "Irrigation scheduling must factor in rainfall since addition of an irrigation sequence on already saturated media will leach fertilizer and possibly contribute to ground water pollution."
- Non-uniform soil under one irrigation zone is documented: Alabama Extension found every one of nine nurseries below the 80 percent uniformity guideline, with "0.1 inches of irrigation while others received 0.7 inches" in one block, in the research note, not checked by the planner.
- So whether rain helps or harms is a fact about one bed at one hour, and a roof that decides for the whole house gets it wrong for some of them.

## The finding that changes the rain story

A cloth shade house already lets all the rain through, onto every bed.
So in the setting the team chose, admitting rain is not the new act.
The new act is shedding a storm from a bed that is already wet, where the Apopka report says the water leaches fertilizer and may pollute ground water.
The demo already shows it: in the storm on 3 June the blueberry zone stays shut, with the words "Shut: the soil is wet enough".
That zone has been narrated as the dull one, and it may be the most valuable one.
The line on offer, from the water note: "The irrigation system stays. The roof decides whether today's rain is used or thrown away, bed by bed."
Rain sensors today decide whether the pump runs after rain, and none of them decides whether the rain lands.

## The team lead's hypotheses, each with its verdict

| Hypothesis | Verdict | Why | What to say in its place |
|---|---|---|---|
| Miles of irrigation infrastructure are wasted, because we can depend on rain | Contradicted | The wet season brings about 60 percent of the year's rain and eight months are dry, "daily water applications are typical in nurseries", and no source describes a nursery without irrigation. The team's own run has rain supplying about 54 percent for one zone and none for another | "The irrigation system stays. The roof decides whether today's rain is used or thrown away." |
| Overhead irrigation is about 25 percent efficient, so the roof helps | Not ours | The figure is real, UF/IFAS AE263, but rain falls between the pots just as a sprinkler's water does, and the documented fix is drippers | Nothing. Never use this figure as something the roof fixes |
| Energy is spent on motors | Contradicted, and it cuts against the team | Heating is 70 to 80 percent of a northern grower's energy, fans are the large electrical load, and Cravo's own figure for a roof is 3 to 5 kWh per hectare per day. A roof of many fins has more actuators than one curtain | Nothing about motors. Cost of many actuators stays on the "not claimed" list |
| Less spent on artificial lighting | Contradicted for this setting | The US Department of Energy reports lamps are "less common" in the sunny south east and assumed only in rigid glass houses, UF's foliage guides manage light by shade alone, lamps run on dark days when any roof is already open, and the houses that use lamps keep rain out on purpose | Nothing about lighting |
| Three crops under one roof is not realistic | Half right | A mixed house is plausible and not proven common. But the need behind it is the Apopka centre's own table, and stage and season make it true for a single crop | Reframe as light classes, stages, and seasons, see the scenarios below |
| Heat and cooling | Real problem, weak link | Fans use "0.5 to 1 kilowatt hour/sq ft/year" and open roofs hold "within a degree or two of outside temperature", but that argues for open roofs in general | One sentence at most, as context, never as a saving |

## Hero scenarios, ranked

1. **One foliage nursery near Apopka, beds at different stages.**
   A weeping fig grown bright for its trunk, then held under heavy shade for months before sale, beside shade crops that never leave 80 percent.
   It answers the judge who says "just build two houses", because that is exactly what the grower does today.
2. **One foliage nursery, three light classes, all from the one Apopka table.**
   Three zones stay on the screen, and the words become "three light classes".
   The Boston fern is already in that table at "70 - 80%", so zone A stands as it is.
3. **One crop over one year, as a supporting beat only.**
   The croton line, 30 percent not enough in summer and 47 percent too much in winter.

## What this does not change, and what it would cost if it did

- The simulation's three crops and their numbers stay as built tonight.
  They rest on Purdue's daily light table and were verified against the real data, and the Apopka table gives shade percent and foot candles, which nobody has converted to a daily light sum.
  Swapping a crop inside H1 means new parameters and new expected values, which is a replanning of a package that is being built now.
- So the recommendation is a change of words and not of code: the problem is stated generally from the Apopka sources, and the three zones are presented as three light classes, with the crops named as the three whose published daily light figures the team could source.
- New crops can come in later without new physics through the shade share rule the blueberry already uses: croton at 0.30 to 0.47 and pothos at 0.63 to 0.80 are sourced shade shares, and they suit the judge's crop menu in the prospective sandbox, package H4.
- The blueberry stays the weakest zone, and the pitch already says its figure is from Washington State.

## Not found, by any of the three notes

- Any example of shading zoned at the scale of a bed, in research or practice. The words stay "we found no example in our searches".
- Any study that measures the light or growth lost by shading a whole house for its most sensitive crop.
- The labour of moving plants between houses or changing cloth, as a figure.
- A primary source that says in words that growers build separate houses at different shade percentages, though the tables and the cloth used in the trials are consistent with it.
- A price for nursery water in Florida.
