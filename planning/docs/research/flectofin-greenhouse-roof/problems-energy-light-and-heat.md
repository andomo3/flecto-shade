# Does a bed by bed roof save energy on motors, lighting, or cooling, and what do the sources say about where the energy goes?

## Answer

Motor energy is CONTRADICTED: roof and screen drives are a rounding error next to heating, fans, and lamps, and a roof of many fins has more actuators than one curtain, not fewer.
Saving on artificial lighting is CONTRADICTED for the team's own setting: no source shows lamps over Central Florida foliage in shade houses, and the DOE says lamps are "less common" in the sunny southeast.
The lighting argument is PLAUSIBLE only for heated northern glasshouses, and those are the closed houses that keep rain out, so the lighting story and the rain story belong to different buildings.
Light thrown away by coarse shade is STRONG in time and PLAUSIBLE in space: a Clemson figure shows fixed shade delivering 8.5 mol a day where a moving shade delivered 11.5, but existing curtains already fix that, and nobody was found who quantified the loss from shading a whole house for its most sensitive crop.
Heat is STRONG as a problem and PLAUSIBLE as a link: fans use 0.5 to 1 kWh per sq ft per year, open roofs avoid most of it, and shade lowers canopy temperature, but open roofs already exist and the team has no figure of its own.
The best honest line is a UF/IFAS sentence on croton: "30% shade is not quite enough in summer, but 47% is too much in winter."
That is a grower's fixed shade being wrong for the season, in Florida, in the extension service's own words, and it supports the size and timing of the decision without any energy claim.

Found by a research agent that opened every source on 2026-09-19.
Anything it saw only in a search snippet is marked unverified and is not used in a verdict.
Where a line says "derived", the arithmetic is the agent's, from two quoted figures, and is never said out loud as a source's finding.

## Findings

### Where the energy goes: heating, then electricity, and the drives are not even listed

- University of Wisconsin Extension, written for northern growers: "Heating energy represents 70 to 80% of a typical greenhouse grower's total energy consumption, while electricity represents 10 to 15% and transportation fuel (for trucks, tractors, and other vehicles) makes up the rest."
- The same source: "Energy costs are the third highest cost for most greenhouse growers, behind labor and plant materials", and they have "grown to over 10%" of sales.
- The same source on electricity: "Ventilation fans are a substantial user of electricity in a greenhouse."
- Its chapters on saving energy cover glazing, infiltration, curtains, heaters, fans, and lamps. Curtain and vent motors are never named as an energy cost.
- A comparable breakdown for a Florida shade house or greenhouse was not found.
- In Florida the heating share is small and the cooling share is large, by the logic of the UF/IFAS design bulletin below, but no source gave a percentage, so none is stated.

### Motors: a rounding error, and the hypothesis cuts against the team

- Cravo, the maker, on its own site: "Powered by as little as 5 kWh per day, Cravo's automatic retractable roofs are not only solar-compatible but also offer seamless remote operation", and "Sustainability-powered - 3-5 kWh day/ha". This is a maker's claim.
- Cravo at a trade event, 2017, on its cooling house: the "ventilation system uses <2 kilowatt per hour of electricity per hectare per day". The unit is garbled in the original, and it is a maker's claim.
- UMass and the extension service on fans: "Fan ventilation can use from 0.5 to 1 kilowatt hour/sq ft/year."
- Derived: 3 to 5 kWh a day per hectare is about 1,100 to 1,800 kWh a year over about 107,600 sq ft, or 0.01 to 0.02 kWh per sq ft per year. That is about 1 to 3 percent of what the same source says fans use.
- Derived: DOE gives HID lamps at "11 W/ft2" run "an average of 2,000 hours per year", which is about 22 kWh per sq ft per year where lamps are used. Roof drives are about a thousandth of that.
- A small curtain motor sold to growers is rated "Wattage: 100W", "Output Speed: 3 RPM", and handles "up to 1,750 sq ft on a single bar curtain". Drives are small and run for minutes a day.
- The existing research file already records that "one drive motor can handle up to 40,000 sq ft of screen material". One motor for 40,000 sq ft is the low energy, low part count design. A roof of many fins in many zones needs more actuators, not fewer.
- No source was found that names roof or screen motor electricity as a cost growers want to cut.

### Supplemental lighting: a real and large cost, but a northern and glasshouse cost

- DOE, 2020: "In 2019, horticultural lighting installations in the U.S. consumed 9.6 terawatt hours (TWh) of site electricity", of which "13% from supplemental lighting in greenhouses".
- DOE on area and hours: "supplemented greenhouses have the largest total area, at 56.6 million square feet", and "Electric lighting used to supplement sunlight in greenhouses is operated an average of 2,000 hours per year."
- DOE on intensity: supplemented greenhouses have "high-intensity discharge (HID) lighting consuming 11 W/ft2 and LED lighting consuming 7 W/ft2", and "lighting in supplemented greenhouses consumed 1,202 GWh of site electricity".
- DOE on where: "In northern states where daylight hours shorten significantly for winter months, or in the Pacific Northwest where it is often rainy, supplemental lighting in greenhouses is more common. In the sunny southeastern and southwestern states, supplemental lighting in greenhouses is less common."
- DOE on which houses: "it is assumed that supplemental lighting is only used in permanent rigid plastic and glass greenhouse structures that operate year-round, and not in plastic film covered structures."
- Purdue Extension: "The percentage of U.S. greenhouse acreage using supplemental lighting is increasing, but it is still low - estimated at 10 to 20 percent in the northern half of the United States." The dash replaces the original's long dash.
- Purdue on cost: "In 2004, it was estimated that the average cost of supplemental lighting was $0.052 per square foot per week across the United States." The figure is old.
- Purdue on when it pays: "supplemental lighting is generally worthwhile only when increased photosynthesis leads to greater revenue (such as more turns of plugs, cutting liners or more cut flowers)."
- Purdue on the need: "Without supplemental photosynthetic lighting, greenhouse crops in the northern half of the United States often receive insufficient light (<10 mol m-2 d-1) for several months of the year."
- University of Georgia, in Plants, 2024: "supplemental lighting is commonly applied in winter, but this practice has a significant energy cost, accounting for 10-30% of operating expenses."
- The same paper on control: "Greenhouse lights are traditionally adjusted based on sunlight intensity to meet crops' daily light requirements", and it found "the DLI requirement can be reduced by approximately 5.25 mol m-2 d-1 on the day following a sunny day."
- Wisconsin Extension on the controller: "use a light integral controller. This type of controller measures the sunlight entering the greenhouse daily and controls the lights to provide just enough supplemental light to reach a minimum daily light integral."
- So a daily light target drives lamp hours directly, which the existing research file already shows for Argus.
- Michigan State's pages by Runkle and Lopez, and the Faust and Logan paper in HortScience, blocked the agent's reader. They are unverified, snippet only, and are not used here.
- A Wageningen figure for the share of Dutch glasshouse electricity that goes to lamps was not found in the time given.

### Does a moving roof cut lamp hours? An argument, not a finding

- Lamps run when sunlight is short: in winter, on dull days, in the north.
- On those days a shade roof is already fully open, so bed by bed shading has nothing to give back.
- The only route to lamp savings is structural: a fin roof must block less light when open than the cloth, cables, and bundled curtain it replaces.
- Purdue says structure matters: greenhouse light can be "40 to 70 percent lower because of shading from greenhouse glazing, structures, and hanging baskets."
- No source was found on the open state light transmission of a louvre or fin roof against a parked curtain. Not found.
- A fin that parks edge on to the sky still casts a shadow, and the team's fins do not track the sun, so this may cut against the roof. It is unknown.

### Light lost to shade that is too coarse: quantified in time, not found in space

- Jim Faust, Clemson University, 2004, on one clear day: "The daily light integral delivered was 34.2 moles/day outdoors, 17.1 moles/day in the greenhouse without shade, 11.5 moles/day in the greenhouse with retractable shade, and 8.5 moles/day in the greenhouse with fixed shade."
- Faust again: "Thus, retractable shade allowed for 35 percent more light to be delivered compared to the fixed shade greenhouse."
- Faust on the asymmetry: "Whitewash can always be added to reduce light levels for propagation or low light crops; however if the curtain provides excess shade, there is no easy way to provide higher light levels."
- UF/IFAS on croton in Florida: "Suggested shade percentages for stem production in Florida are 30 to 47%. For most cultivars, 30% shade is not quite enough in summer, but 47% is too much in winter."
- UF/IFAS on pothos: "Pothos should be grown with levels of 63 to 80% shade (3250 to 2500 ft-c)."
- Derived: croton wants 30 to 47 percent shade and pothos wants 63 to 80 percent. Under one fixed cloth, one of them gets the wrong light all year. That is the team's three crops problem in the extension service's own numbers.
- The existing research file records that coatings cover a whole roof for a season.
- What was not found: any study that quantifies the light or growth lost by shading a whole house, or a whole curtain bay, for its most sensitive crop. Searches for zoned shade, mixed crops, and compromise shade levels returned makers' pages and no data.
- So the time half of the problem is quantified and is already solved by moving curtains. The space half is real in the crop guides and is not quantified by anyone found.

### Heat: fans are the electricity cost, open roofs already avoid it, and shade helps

- UF/IFAS, Florida Greenhouse Design: "Shading is used to control light and lower interior temperatures", and "The shade cloth should be mounted on the exterior side of the glazing for the greatest reduction in the heat load."
- The same bulletin: "Forced convection using fans can lower air temperatures 5°F to 10°F below those found in greenhouses cooled by natural convection only", and natural ventilation needs "A total vent area equivalent to 15 to 30 percent of the floor area".
- The same bulletin on the team's setting: "The simplest type is the shade structure composed of shade cloth supported on poles. This type is inexpensive but is only practical in warm climates." Such a structure has no fans and no heater, so it has almost no energy bill to cut.
- UMass, John Bartok, on open roofs: "During warm weather, the temperature inside the greenhouse can be maintained within a degree or two of outside temperature with little or no energy needed."
- Wisconsin Extension: "Open-roof greenhouse designs can often control the greenhouse temperature sufficiently without exhaust fans", and "Shade curtains can lower air temperatures under the curtains by 10°F."
- Extension on shade and fans: applying external shade can "reduce summer fan operation time".
- UF/IFAS on pothos heat limits: growth suffers when "day temperatures routinely exceed 95°F".
- UF/IFAS on Florida greenhouse tomatoes: "it is best to manage shading so that day temperatures are no higher than 88°F to 90°F for tomatoes", with evaporative pads giving "an additional few degrees of cooling".
- Cravo claims its roofs "save in expensive electricity costs for cooling" and gives no figure. A maker's claim.
- A Van Wingerden figure for cooling energy was not found.

### Central Florida shade houses: no lamps found, so no lamp bill to cut

- The UF/IFAS production guides opened for pothos and croton manage light only by shade percentage, and mention no supplemental lighting for production.
- DOE assumes lamps are "not in plastic film covered structures", and a cloth on poles is a step below that.
- Purdue puts typical foliage and shade crops in the low and medium light classes, and notes that "6 to 12 mol m-2 d-1 are medium-light crops".
- Outdoor light "vary from 5 to 60 mol m-2 d-1" across the country and year, per Purdue, and Florida sits at the bright end. A Florida specific winter figure was not opened, so none is given.
- Where the lamp argument would apply: heated glass or rigid plastic houses in the northern half of the country, the Pacific Northwest, Canada, and northern Europe, growing plugs and liners, cut flowers, tomatoes, cucumbers, and leafy greens.
- Could those houses admit rain? No. The existing research file shows closed houses keep rain off the crop on purpose, and they are heated, so an open roof in lamp season loses heat, which is 70 to 80 percent of their energy.
- So no single house was found where both the lamp saving and the rain admission apply.
