# What real, sourced statistics describe the wider pattern behind a farmer who could not get water to her land, and which of them may a public landing page use?

## Answer

Nine statistics came back STRONG, which here means a primary document that was opened on 2026-09-20 with a figure, a year and a definition that survives a critic.
The single best one for a landing page headline is the World Bank's own table: in 2013, 20.6 percent of the world's cultivated land was equipped for irrigation, and in sub-Saharan Africa the figure was 3.4 percent.
That one line carries the family story outward without any exaggeration, it is a measured infrastructure count rather than a model, and the two numbers sit side by side in one table in one report that was read page by page.

Three widely repeated figures the team would plausibly have reached for are UNUSABLE or WEAK, and the report below says which and why.
The biggest trap is "smallholders feed 70 to 80 percent of the world", which the peer reviewed literature has specifically refuted.
The second is the US$27.3 billion a year cost of salt damage, which is real but which no opened primary source would give up: every route to the paper was refused, so it traces only to press releases written by the authors' own institutes.
The third is FAO's own disagreement with itself about how much irrigated land goes out of production each year, where a 1997 FAO bulletin says 2 to 3 million hectares and a 2002 FAO book says 0.25 to 0.5 million hectares.

One finding shapes the whole page.
FAO's 1997 bulletin states in plain words that estimates of irrigation-damaged land "have ranged from 10 to 48% of worldwide total irrigated area", and FAO's own 2024 assessment puts the figure at 10 percent while saying the uncertainty is high.
So the honest register for this page is the low end with the range named, never the dramatic end.

### Provenance of the quotes

Quotes marked "read directly" were read by this agent from the document's own pages, as page images, on 2026-09-20.
That covers the World Bank 2020 report and the USDA ERS irrigation chapter.
Every other quote came back through a page reader that returns the page's words, so check the wording against the page before it goes on a slide.
Two source sentences contain an em dash in the original.
House style forbids the character, so it has been written as a plain dash and the substitution is flagged at each quote.

## Theme 1: land lost or degraded because of irrigation problems

### 1.1 The total area of salt-affected soil, FAO 2024. STRONG

- Figure: 1 381 million hectares, or 10.7 percent of the total global land area, as assessed in the report published 11 December 2024.
- Quote: "The report estimates the area of salt-affected soils at 1 381 million ha (Mha), or 10.7 percent of the total global land area."
- FAO, "FAO launches first major global assessment of salt-affected soils in 50 years", FAO Newsroom, 11 December 2024, reporting the report `Global status of salt-affected soils` (FAO, 2024, document code cd3044en).
  https://www.fao.org/newsroom/detail/fao-launches-first-major-global-assessment-of-salt-affected-soils-in-50-years/en
- Critic's line: this is all land, not cropland, and a large part of it is naturally saline ground that no irrigation ever touched, so it is not a measure of damage done by farming.
- Note on access: the report PDF itself returned HTTP 403 by every route tried, so these figures were read from FAO's own news release about its own report, not from the report.

### 1.2 The share of irrigated cropland affected by salinity, FAO 2024. STRONG

- Figure: 10 percent of irrigated cropland and 10 percent of rainfed cropland, 2024 assessment.
- Quote: "10 percent of irrigated cropland and 10 percent of rainfed cropland are affected by salinity"
- Same source as 1.1.
- Critic's line: FAO attaches its own caveat that uncertainty remains high because of limited data, and this 10 percent is half the 20 percent figure that older work reports, so the two must never be mixed in one paragraph.

### 1.3 Land at risk and the modelled future, FAO 2024. WEAK

- Figure: an additional one billion hectares at risk; affected area may reach 24 to 32 percent of the total land surface under current warming trends.
- Quote: "an additional one billion hectares at risk due to the climate crisis and human mismanagement"
- Quote: "Models of global aridity trends indicate that, under the existing trend of temperature increase, the affected area may increase to between 24 and 32 percent of the total land surface."
- Same source as 1.1.
- Critic's line: "at risk" is never defined on the page and the 24 to 32 percent is a model projection of aridity, not an observation, so both are forecasts dressed as counts.

### 1.4 How much irrigated land goes out of production each year, FAO 2002. STRONG for the figure, with an age caveat

- Figure: 0.25 to 0.5 million hectares lost from production every year to salt build-up, and 20 to 30 million hectares seriously damaged, stated in 2002.
- Quote: "0.25-0.5 million hectares are estimated to be lost from production every year as a result of salt build-up."
- Quote: "At present, about 20-30 million hectares of irrigated land are seriously damaged by the build-up of salts"
- FAO, `Crops and drops: making the best use of water for agriculture`, Rome, 2002, chapter page.
  https://www.fao.org/4/y3918e/y3918e10.htm
- Critic's line: the book is 24 years old, it names no study for the figure, and "seriously damaged" is not defined on the page.

### 1.5 The share of irrigated land affected, FAO 2002. WEAK

- Figure: about 30 percent of irrigated land severely or moderately affected; salinization reducing the irrigated area by 1 to 2 percent a year.
- Quote: "About 30 percent of irrigated land is now severely or moderately affected."
- Quote: "The salinization of irrigated areas is reducing the existing area under irrigation by 1-2 percent a year."
- Same source as 1.4.
- Critic's line: "severely or moderately" is undefined, and a 1 to 2 percent annual reduction compounded over the 24 years since would have removed a quarter of world irrigation, which did not happen.

### 1.6 FAO's own statement that the estimates are not settled, FAO 1997. STRONG, and the most useful sentence on this page

- Figure: published estimates of irrigation-damaged land span 10 to 48 percent of world irrigated area, as of 1997.
- Quote: "Estimates of the area affected have ranged from 10 to 48% of worldwide total irrigated area."
- FAO, `Irrigation potential in Africa: A basin approach`, FAO Land and Water Bulletin 4, Land and Water Development Division, Rome, 1997, ISBN 92-5-103966-6, Chapter 7.
  https://www.fao.org/4/w4347e/w4347e10.htm
- Critic's line: it is a 1997 literature spread rather than a measurement, which is precisely why it should be quoted, because it is the sentence that stops anyone treating any single figure as settled.

### 1.7 FAO 1997 on annual loss, which contradicts FAO 2002. WEAK, flagged deliberately

- Figure: 2 to 3 million hectares going out of production worldwide each year, as of 1997.
- Quote: "About 2 to 3 million ha are going out of production worldwide each year due to salinity problems."
- Same source as 1.6.
- Critic's line: this is roughly five to ten times FAO's own 2002 figure in 1.4, and no source found reconciles the two, so citing either one alone is choosing a number rather than reporting one.

### 1.8 Farm-level effect of irrigation-induced salinity, FAO 1997. WEAK

- Figure: paddy and wheat yields around 50 percent lower on degraded soils, and net incomes around 85 percent lower on salt-affected land.
- Quote: "Comprehensive studies of farm-level effects of irrigation-induced salinity indicate that the yields of paddy and wheat are around 50% lower on the degraded soils and net incomes in salt-affected lands are around 85% lower than the unaffected land."
- Same source as 1.6.
- Critic's line: the page names no study, no country and no year for the underlying work, so "comprehensive studies" cannot be checked.

### 1.9 The economic cost of salt-induced degradation, Qadir et al. 2014. WEAK, and say so if used

- Figure: about US$27.3 billion a year in lost crop production; about 62 million hectares, or 20 percent of the world's irrigated land; an average 2 000 hectares a day over more than 20 years across 75 countries; US$441 per hectare in 2013 money.
- Quote, from IWMI's press release, not from the paper: "an average of 2,000 hectares of irrigated land in arid and semi-arid areas across 75 countries have been degraded by salt"
- Quote, same release, em dash replaced with a plain dash per house style: "Today an area the size of France is affected - about 62 million hectares (20%) of the world's irrigated lands"
- Quote, same release: "The inflation-adjusted cost of salt-induced land degradation in 2013 was estimated at $441 per hectare"
- IWMI, "Press Release: World Losing 2,000 Hectares of Irrigated Farmland to Salt-Induced Degradation Daily", 28 October 2014.
  https://www.iwmi.org/news/press-release-world-losing-2000-hectares-irrigated-farmland-salt-induced-degradation-daily/
- Underlying paper, NOT opened: Qadir, M., Quillerou, E., Nangia, V., Murtaza, G., Singh, M., Thomas, R. J., Drechsel, P., and Noble, A. D. (2014). Economics of salt-induced land degradation and restoration. Natural Resources Forum, 38(4), 282-295. https://doi.org/10.1111/1477-8947.12054
- Critic's line: the paper is paywalled and Wiley, HAL, CGSpace, ResearchGate and the McMaster repository all refused access on 2026-09-20, so the figure traces only to press releases written by the authors' own institutes, and the CGSpace record describes the US$27.3 billion as arrived at by simple extrapolation.
- If the team uses it at all, the attribution must read "Qadir et al. 2014, as reported by IWMI", and it should not carry a headline.

## Theme 2: smallholder access to irrigation

### 2.1 The share of the world's cultivated land equipped for irrigation, World Bank 2020. STRONG

- Figure: in 2013, area equipped for irrigation as a percentage of cultivated land was 20.6 percent for the world, 3.4 percent for sub-Saharan Africa, 40.9 percent for Asia, 45.7 percent for South Asia and 56.0 percent for South East and East Asia.
- Figure: total area equipped for irrigation, world, was 196.1 Mha in 1973, 265.9 Mha in 1993 and 325.1 Mha in 2013; for sub-Saharan Africa, 4.2, 6.1 and 8.2 Mha.
- Quote, read directly from the table source note: "Data are adapted from AQUASTAT (database), Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, Rome www.fao.org/nr/aquastat."
- Quote, read directly from the body text: "Data from the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, summarized in table 2.2, suggest global irrigated area to have increased from 196 million hectares to 328 million hectares between 1973 and 2013 when total area wetted by groundwater was placed at 127 million hectares."
- Shah, Tushaar, Regassa Namara, and Abhishek Rajan. 2020. `Accelerating Irrigation Expansion in Sub-Saharan Africa: Policy Lessons from the Global Revolution in Farmer-Led Smallholder Irrigation`. World Bank, Washington, DC, Water Global Practice, Table 2.2 on page 11.
  https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/479941624264066924/pdf/Accelerating-Irrigation-Expansion-in-Sub-Saharan-Africa-Policy-Lessons-from-the-Global-Revolution-in-Farmer-Led-Smallholder-Irrigation.pdf
- Critic's line: "area equipped for irrigation" counts the infrastructure that exists, not the land actually watered in a given year, and the table's data year is 2013, so it is now more than a decade old.
- Read directly from the PDF pages by this agent.

### 2.2 Sub-Saharan Africa's irrigated share of agricultural land, World Bank 2020. STRONG, with an internal inconsistency worth knowing

- Figure: about 5 percent of agricultural land, 6 million hectares, equipped for irrigation, growing at about 3 percent a year.
- Quote, read directly: "Despite huge scope and need, the pace of growth of such smallholder irrigation in Sub-Saharan Africa has remained tepid at about 3 percent per year and just 5 percent of agricultural land (6 million hectares) is equipped for irrigation (FAO 2011a; Makin 2016)."
- Quote, read directly: "Sub-Saharan Africa is adding about 60,000 hectares per year to its stock of smallholder irrigation, and this too remains concentrated in a few countries."
- Same source as 2.1, page 6.
- Critic's line: this same report gives 3.4 percent of cultivated land in Table 2.2 and 5 percent of agricultural land here, because agricultural land includes pasture, so the page must name its denominator or drop the figure.

### 2.3 How little water sub-Saharan Africa uses for irrigation, World Bank 2020. STRONG

- Figure: sub-Saharan Africa withdraws about 3 percent of its water resources for irrigation, against 33 percent for South and Southeast Asia, 2009 data.
- Quote, read directly: "Sub-Saharan Africa withdraws barely 3 percent of its water resources for irrigation, as compared with 33 percent for South and Southeast Asia (table 1.2)."
- Quote, read directly: "In nearly 40 years, from 1970 to 2009, Sub-Saharan Africa added just 3.1 million hectares of irrigation, whereas South and Southeast Asia added 48.5 million hectares, almost wholly by growing groundwater irrigation."
- Same source as 2.1, page 6, drawing on AQUASTAT as accessed 25 July 2013.
- Critic's line: a low withdrawal share means water is available on paper, not that it is reachable by any particular farmer, which is a different claim entirely.

### 2.4 How far people live from water, World Bank 2020 citing World Bank 2018. WEAK, and the closest figure to the family story

- Figure: an estimated 85 percent of sub-Saharan Africa's population lives more than 10 kilometres from a major river or lake.
- Quote, read directly: "An estimated 85 percent of Sub-Saharan Africa's population lives more than 10 kilometers from a major river or lake, and they are unlikely to benefit from recession or gravity irrigation (World Bank 2018)."
- Same source as 2.1, page 5.
- Critic's line: it counts people, not farmland, and it is a second-hand citation to a 2018 World Bank source that this research did not open, so it is reported evidence rather than measured evidence.
- This is the sentence closest to "she could not get water to it", and it must be attributed as "World Bank 2020, citing World Bank 2018".

### 2.5 The irrigation leverage figure, World Bank 2020 citing You et al. 2011. WEAK

- Figure: 7 million irrigated hectares out of 175 million cultivated hectares account for 38 percent of total agricultural output in sub-Saharan Africa.
- Quote, read directly: "Sub-Saharan Africa's own experience highlights the criticality of irrigation: 7 million irrigated hectares out of 175 million cultivated hectares account for 38 percent of total agricultural output (You et al. 2011)."
- Same source as 2.1, page 6.
- Critic's line: 4 percent of the land producing 38 percent of output is an extraordinary ratio resting on one modelling study that this research did not open, and irrigated land is also the better land, so the comparison is not like for like.

### 2.6 Irrigation's share of global food, World Bank topic page. WEAK

- Figure: irrigated agriculture covers about 20 percent of farmland and produces about 40 percent of global food; 6 percent of farmland in sub-Saharan Africa is irrigated; 80 percent of world agriculture is rainfed.
- Quote: "In Sub-Saharan Africa, however, only 6% of farmland is irrigated, far below what's needed to meet surging food demand."
- Quote: "By contrast, irrigated agriculture covers just 20% of farmland yet produces 40% of global food."
- Quote, em dash replaced with a plain dash per house style: "Today, 80% of global agriculture is rainfed, producing 60% of our food - but leaving 500 million smallholder farmers highly vulnerable to erratic rainfall and limited access to inputs and finance."
- World Bank, "Water for Food", topic page.
  https://www.worldbank.org/ext/en/topic/water/water-for-food
- Critic's line: an undated web page that cites no data year, no definition and no underlying source for any of the four figures, which is why it is WEAK despite coming from the right organisation.
- The 20 percent of farmland is consistent with 2.1, so prefer 2.1 and use this page only for the 40 percent of food, saying it is a World Bank web figure.

### 2.7 The share of farms that are smallholdings, Lowder, Skoet and Raney 2016. WEAK, secondary only

- Figure: about 84 percent of the world's roughly 570 million farms are smaller than two hectares, and they operate about 12 percent of the world's farmland.
- Quote, from Our World in Data, not from the paper: "Most (84%) of the world's 570 million farms are smallholdings; that is, farms less than two hectares in size."
- Underlying paper, NOT opened: Lowder, S. K., Skoet, J., and Raney, T. (2016). The number, size, and distribution of farms, smallholder farms, and family farms worldwide. World Development, 87, 16-29. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2015.10.041
- Reached through: Our World in Data, "Smallholders produce one-third of the world's food, less than half of what many headlines claim".
  https://ourworldindata.org/smallholder-food-production
- Critic's line: two hectares is an arbitrary cut-off that Our World in Data itself flags, the underlying data are national agricultural censuses of different years, and this research could not open the paper because the publisher returned HTTP 403.

### 2.8 The share of food smallholders produce, Ricciardi et al. 2018. WEAK as a source, but it is the figure that kills a popular myth

- Figure: farms under two hectares produce 28 to 31 percent of total crop production and 30 to 34 percent of food supply, on 24 percent of gross agricultural area.
- Quote, from a search summary of the paper's abstract, not read in the paper: "Farms under 2 hectares globally produce 28-31% of total crop production and 30-34% of food supply on 24% of gross agricultural area."
- Underlying paper, NOT opened: Ricciardi, V., Ramankutty, N., Mehrabi, Z., Jarvis, L., and Chookolingo, B. (2018). How much of the world's food do smallholders produce? Global Food Security, 17, 64-72. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.gfs.2018.05.002
- Critic's line: the paper covers 154 crops in 55 countries and not the whole world, and this research reached it only through a search summary and Our World in Data because ScienceDirect returned HTTP 403.
- Its value here is defensive: it is the study that refuted "smallholders feed 70 to 80 percent of the world", so the team must know it exists even if it never prints it.

## Theme 3: water applied in the wrong place or at the wrong time

### 3.1 How much irrigation water is consumed without feeding a crop, Jagermeyr et al. 2015. STRONG

- Figure: global irrigation withdrawal 2 469 cubic kilometres, consumption 1 257 cubic kilometres, of which 608 cubic kilometres non-beneficially consumed, averaged over 2004 to 2009.
- Figure: beneficial irrigation efficiency below 30 percent in south Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, above 60 percent in Europe and North America.
- Quote: "We arrive at an estimate of global irrigation water withdrawal of 2469 km3 (2004-2009 average); irrigation water consumption is calculated to be 1257 km3, of which 608 km3 are non-beneficially consumed, i.e., lost through evaporation, interception, and conveyance."
- Quote: "We find pronounced regional patterns in beneficial irrigation efficiency (a refined irrigation efficiency indicator accounting for crop-productive water consumption only), due to differences in these features, with the lowest values (< 30 %) in south Asia and sub-Saharan Africa and the highest values (> 60 %) in Europe and North America."
- Quote: "Replacing surface systems by sprinkler or drip systems could, on average across the world's river basins, reduce the non-beneficial consumption at river basin level by 54 and 76 %, respectively, while maintaining the current level of crop yields."
- Jagermeyr, J., Gerten, D., Heinke, J., Schaphoff, S., Kummu, M., and Lucht, W. (2015). Water savings potentials of irrigation systems: global simulation of processes and linkages. Hydrology and Earth System Sciences, 19, 3073-3091. https://doi.org/10.5194/hess-19-3073-2015
  https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/19/3073/2015/
- Critic's line: every figure is an output of the LPJmL model rather than a measurement, and "non-beneficial" counts water that evaporates, not water that percolates into an aquifer and is pumped again downstream.
- 608 of 1 257 cubic kilometres is 48 percent, and that arithmetic is this agent's, not the paper's.

### 3.2 Field application efficiency by system type, USDA ERS. STRONG

- Figure: gravity-flow field application efficiencies typically 40 to 65 percent, up to 85 percent with improved systems; sprinkler systems 50 to 95 percent, most in the range 75 to 85 percent. US data, 1998 survey vintage.
- Quote, read directly: "Water losses are comparatively high under traditional gravity-flow systems due to percolation losses below the crop-root zone, and water runoff at the end of the field. Field application efficiencies typically range from 40-65 percent (Negri and Hanchar), although improved systems with proper management may achieve efficiencies of up to 85 percent (USDA, 1997)."
- Quote, read directly: "Field application efficiencies for properly designed and operated sprinkler systems range from 50-95 percent, with most systems in the range of 75-85 percent (USDA, 1997)."
- USDA Economic Research Service, `Agricultural Resources and Environmental Indicators`, Chapter 2.2, Irrigation Water Management, pages 7 and 8.
  https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/publications/41964/30287_irrigationwater.pdf
- Critic's line: these are United States figures resting on the 1998 Farm and Ranch Irrigation Survey, and at field scale a "loss" is often water that recharges an aquifer rather than water destroyed.
- Read directly from the PDF pages by this agent.
- [gap: the chapter PDF does not print its edition year. ERS lists the 2003 edition under publication id 41965 while this chapter sits under 41964, and its references run to 2001, so the edition is probably the 2000 edition, but that was not confirmed.]

### 3.3 The definitions that keep an over-watering claim honest, USDA ERS. STRONG

- Quote, read directly: "Application efficiency is the ratio of the average depth of irrigation water infiltrated and stored in the root zone for crop consumptive use to the average depth applied, expressed as a percentage (USDA, 1997)."
- Quote, read directly: "The leaching requirement, which accounts for the major difference between irrigation efficiency and application efficiency, is the quantity of water required to flush soil salts below the plant root zone."
- Quote, read directly: "Field-level losses include surface runoff at the end of the field, deep percolation below the crop-root zone (not used for leaching), and excess evaporation from soil and water surfaces."
- Same source as 3.2, page 3.
- Critic's line: this is the source's own definition rather than a finding, and the second quote is the one that complicates the story, because some deliberate over-application is required to flush salt out of the root zone.
- Any page that says "over-watering causes salt" without this sentence is telling half the truth, since under-watering concentrates salt and the grower is steering between two failures.

### 3.4 Wrong time, stated as conventional versus improved practice, USDA ERS. STRONG as a framing, no number

- Quote, read directly from Table 2.2.1, the row "Timing of applied water", conventional practice then improved practice: "Fixed calendar schedule." and "Water applied as needed by crop; managed for profit (not yield); managed for improved effectiveness of rainfall."
- Quote, read directly from the same table, the row "Assessing crop needs": "Judgment estimates." and "Soil moisture monitoring; plant tissue monitoring; weather-based computations."
- Same source as 3.2, page 4.
- Critic's line: it is a qualitative table of what counts as conventional and what counts as improved, and it carries no count of how many farms do either.
- The phrase "managed for improved effectiveness of rainfall" is USDA naming, as an improved practice, exactly the decision this roof makes.

### 3.5 How irrigation reaches water quality, USDA ERS. STRONG, qualitative

- Quote, read directly: "Irrigated agriculture may affect water quality in several ways, including higher chemical use rates associated with irrigated production, increased field salinity and soil erosion due to applied water, accelerated pollutant transport with drainage flows, degradation due to increased deep percolation to saline formations, and greater instream pollutant concentrations due to reduced flows."
- Same source as 3.2, page 1.
- Critic's line: it lists mechanisms, not magnitudes, so it can support "this is how nutrient loss happens" and never "this much nutrient is lost".

### 3.6 What switching to drip saves, FAO 2002. WEAK

- Figure: 30 to 60 percent less water used, stated in 2002.
- Quote: "farmers who switch from furrow (trench) systems or sprinkler irrigation to drip systems can cut their water use by 30 to 60 percent."
- Same source as 1.4, FAO `Crops and drops`, 2002.
- Critic's line: the page names no study, and a cut in water applied is not a cut in water consumed, because saved water is commonly used to irrigate more land.

## What must never be claimed from these figures

Flecto Shade does not bring irrigation to land that has none, and every figure in Theme 2 describes the world the family story came from rather than a problem this roof solves.
A roof needs a structure to sit on, and a farmer with no water line to her hundred acres almost certainly has no shade house either, so these numbers set the stakes and must never be positioned as the gap the product closes.

Flecto Shade does not restore, reverse or prevent soil degradation, and nothing in the simulation models salt, drainage, soil chemistry or crop yield.
The salinity figures in Theme 1 may be used to say why the placement and timing of water matters at all, and they may never be used to imply that this roof takes a hectare out of the damaged column.

None of these figures is a measurement of Flecto Shade.
They come from FAO, the World Bank, USDA and one peer reviewed model, and they describe irrigated agriculture at global scale, while the simulation outputs one decision per bed under modelled weather.
No hectare, no dollar, no percentage and no yield on this page may be attributed to the project, and the page should say in its own words that the numbers are the context and the roof is the proposal.
