# Is irrigation infrastructure wasted when a roof could depend on rain and redistribute it, and what do the sources say about water and rain in nurseries and shade houses?

## Answer

The hypothesis as worded is CONTRADICTED: rain in Central Florida is seasonal, a crop's peak demand is several times the rain that falls, and no source treats irrigation as replaceable by rain.
The irrigation system is not wasted, it is the backstop, and the roof does not remove one pipe.
One smaller claim is STRONG: the FAO's own definition of effective rainfall says rain on wet soil is lost and rain on dry soil saves irrigation, which is exactly the roof's soil rule.
The waste in nursery irrigation is real and quantified, about 75% of overhead water misses the pot, but a roof does not fix it, because rain falls between the pots the same way.
Rain that lands on a covered growing area is piped to ponds or tanks today, harvesting it yields about 65%, and storage costs money, so admitting rain straight onto a dry bed is a PLAUSIBLE shortcut and not a finding.
No source was found that admits rain by zone according to soil moisture, and every maker and extension source on covers keeps rain off the crop.
The honest line is: "the irrigation stays, the roof only decides whether today's rain is counted or thrown away, bed by bed."

Found by a research agent that opened every source on 2026-09-19.
Quotes from AE263 and the FDACS manual were read from the PDF text directly.
The other quotes came through a page reader that returns the page's words, so check the wording against the page before any quote goes on a slide.
Anything seen only in a search snippet is marked unverified and is kept out of the verdicts.

## Findings

### Nurseries use a great deal of water, and Florida regulates it

- UF/IFAS, Chen, Beeson, Stamps, Yeager, and Felter: "on average, 30 to 100 inches of potable water per acre per year may be applied as irrigation."
- The same source: "Water Management Districts have been more scrupulous of water use in nursery crop plant production."
- UMass, Bartok 2009: "greenhouse and nursery crops may need as much as 0.4 gallons per square foot of growing space per day."
- Apopka lies in the St. Johns River Water Management District, whose rule 40C-2.041 requires a consumptive use permit at an "Average annual daily withdrawal equal to or exceeding one hundred thousand (100,000) gallons average per day on an annual basis."
- The same rule also catches "a well in which the outside diameter of the largest permanent water bearing casing is six inches or greater at ground surface."
- The FDACS nursery manual, 2024 edition, FDACS-P-01267: "Contact the appropriate WMD to determine whether a consumptive use or water use permit is required."
- USGS, Florida 2015: "agricultural self-supplied uses, which accounted for 37 percent of the total freshwater withdrawals."
  The report gives no figure for nurseries alone, so that figure is not found.
- A price per gallon for nursery water in Florida was not found.
  Most nursery water is self supplied from wells, so the cost is pumping and the permit, and no source opened put a number on it.

### Overhead irrigation misses the pot most of the time

- UF/IFAS AE263, Haman and Yeager: "The average efficiency of overhead sprinkler systems in the nursery is about 25%."
- The same source: "about 75% of the irrigation water does not get into the container in spite of the fact that scheduling of irrigation events is done properly."
- The same source on the fix: "A well-designed and managed microirrigation system can have an efficiency of 90-95%, meaning that less than 10% of applied water is wasted."
- The same source: "Water recycling ponds are becoming a common part of many nurseries. However, runoff collection is often costly and requires additional land."
- AE263 is marked by UF as an archival copy, so it is a historical figure from UF and not a current recommendation.
- FDACS manual, BMP 2.3.1: "space containers and flats as close as possible to maximize irrigation efficiency."
- FDACS manual: the water needed depends on "the ability of the plant's canopy to capture irrigation water that falls between containers."
- UF/IFAS EP458, Million and Yeager: "Capture factor (CF) describes a plant's capacity to channel water into the container that would otherwise fall between containers."
- Virginia Tech, Owen et al. 2019: one nursery "reduced their water use in 2012 by an average of 43% over the previous three years" by holding a 10 to 20% leaching fraction.
- UF/IFAS on its CIRRIG program, 2015: "A web-based irrigation system saved 21 percent in water use without reducing growth of container-grown landscape plants."
- This cuts against the roof, not for it.
  The loss comes from the gaps between pots, and rain falls into the same gaps.
  The documented fixes are drippers, closer spacing, and leaching fraction tests, and none is a roof.

### Rain on a covered area is piped away, and storing it costs money

- UMass, Bartok 2009: "A 1in. rainfall on an acre of greenhouse amounts to 27,100 gallons."
- The same source: "A common yield is about 65% with losses due to evaporation, wind, leakage."
- The same source on storage: concrete cisterns "about $4,000 to $5,000" for about 5,000 gallons, and fiberglass tanks at "$0.80 to 1.25/ gallon" up to "about 12,000 gallons."
- The same source: "Rain water is generally soft with very few chemicals."
- UMass greenhouse BMP manual: roof runoff sent "to a retention pond or constructed wetland will allow most sediment to settle out before it reaches a brook or stream."
- The same manual: "For gutter-connected greenhouses, consideration should be given to installing a rainwater harvesting system."
- UF/IFAS, Chen et al., ran a trial in Apopka's own industry in which "irrigation water runoff from a landscape plant production bed and stormwater from a greenhouse roof were collected" in a lined basin and reused.
- That trial found the pond water usable except that pH "ranged from 9.3 to 10.3 in pond water", and "Algae in the pond water were an unsightly problem."
- How common roof harvesting is in Florida shade houses was not found.
- A shade house with a porous cloth roof sheds no rain to a gutter at all.
  The rain already falls through, on every bed, wet or dry.
  So for a shade house the roof's new act is keeping rain OFF a wet bed, not letting it onto a dry one.

### Rain cannot replace the irrigation system

- Southwest Florida Water Management District: "Our rainy season is a four-month period from June through September, with average rainfall of about 7.8 inches each month. About 60% of our average annual rainfall is received during that time."
- The same source: "Our dry season is an eight-month period from October through May. During the dry season, our average rainfall is around 3 inches per month."
- That district is the neighbour of Apopka's district, so the figures are regional and not Apopka's own.
- Arithmetic by this agent, not from a source: 0.4 gallons per square foot per day is about 0.64 inches a day, or about 19 inches a month at peak, against 3 to 7.8 inches of rain a month.
- The same arithmetic on the UF range: 30 to 100 inches applied a year, against roughly 55 inches of rain a year, most of it in four months.
- Containers hold little water.
  AE263: "the water available to the plant can be used rapidly, and daily water applications are typical in nurseries."
  A pot that needs water daily cannot wait for a storm.
- No source opened describes a nursery, shade house, or greenhouse that runs without an irrigation system.
- So the infrastructure is not wasted.
  Rain can lower how often it runs, and it cannot remove it.

### Rain counts only when the soil is dry, and this is established practice

- FAO Irrigation and Drainage Paper 25, Dastane 1974, defines effective rainfall as "that portion of total annual or seasonal rainfall which is useful directly and/or indirectly for crop production at the site where it falls but without pumping."
- The same paper: "When a shower falls just after irrigation, it becomes surplus water and is lost through deep percolation or run-off, but if the soil is dry it is recharged with moisture, resulting in a saving in irrigation water."
- The same paper lists what is lost: "surface run-off", "unnecessary deep percolation losses."
- The FDACS nursery manual bases scheduling on "soil water-holding capacity, potential ET rates, and total and projected rainfall", and asks growers to "Manage irrigation based on electronic soil moisture sensors."
- The same manual ties timing to dry leaves: irrigate early so there is "sufficient time for infiltration into the soil and for the plant canopy to dry, thereby reducing evaporative losses and disease development."
- The same manual treats heavy rain as a nutrient loss event: "If rainfall exceeds 3 inches in 3 days or 4 inches in 7 days" a grower may apply extra nitrogen.
- Florida Statute 373.62(1), 2026 text: a person who installs "an automatic landscape irrigation system must properly install, maintain, and operate technology that inhibits or interrupts operation of the system during periods of sufficient moisture."
- That statute covers landscape irrigation, and no source opened applies it to nursery production.
- UF/IFAS AE221, Dukes and Cardenas: the recommended rain sensor set point is at most 1/4 inch.
- UF/IFAS work on CIRRIG adjusts run times from "real-time weather information, including rain."
- A HortScience paper by Million and Yeager, 2020, found that "small daily adjustments to the amount of water applied based on evapotranspiration were not beneficial for saving water compared to adjustments made every 1 to 3 weeks."
  That is a caution: finer control did not save more water in that trial.
- Every one of these practices decides whether the PUMP runs after rain.
  None decides whether the RAIN lands.
  That is the gap the roof's rule sits in.

### Rain admitted by zone according to soil moisture was not found anywhere

- Cravo, December 2024, Richard Vollebregt: "Protecting crops from rain typically requires a poly covered tunnel or a greenhouse, which makes conditions hotter inside."
- The same article: the Auto-Dry rain shelter "helps maintain yield and quality during the rain periods", with materials that "start at US$200,000 per hectare."
- Cravo's page for nursery plants says nothing about using rain on the crop.
- Oregon State Extension on sweet cherry covers, Kaiser, Long, and Brewer 2019: "Retractable row covers shield fruit from rain. They can be highly effective but are costly and labor-intensive."
- The same source: "Automated systems are available, but expensive."
- The same source warns about where shed rain goes: "water running off the row cover can saturate soil in the root zone, resulting in fruit cracking."
- The same source links the harm to soil water: trees under covers "did not develop cracking when soil moisture was kept just below field capacity."
- In every case the cover's job is to keep rain out, and the roof is opened for light and air.
- No source opened admits rain on purpose by bed, by zone, or by soil moisture.
  That is an absence in these searches and not proof.

### Seen only in search snippets, unverified, not used in any verdict

- A container nursery under overhead irrigation "could use between 14,000 to 19,000 gallons of water per acre per day during the peak growing season", NC State, page returned 403.

## What this means for the pitch

- Drop the generalisation to "miles of wasted irrigation infrastructure."
  An expert judge will answer with the dry season in one sentence.
- Keep the claim the size of the decision: a bed, not a building.
  Rain is one more thing decided per bed.
- The strongest honest sentence about rain is the FAO one.
  The same storm is a saving on a dry bed and a loss on a wet bed, and a whole roof cannot tell them apart.
- For a porous shade house the rule reads the other way round, and that may be the better story: today every bed gets every storm, wet or dry, and heavy rain leaches fertiliser.
  The FDACS manual treats 3 inches in 3 days as a leaching event.
  A roof that sheds rain from a bed that is already wet is closer to what the sources worry about than a roof that admits it.
  This is an argument built on the sources and not a finding.
